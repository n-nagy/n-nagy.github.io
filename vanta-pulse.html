Part I

The Product Brief

Intent: align on the core details of the investment area, the problem, why it matters now, and what success looks like.

The problem

Compliance work is fragmented across surfaces that don't share a spine.

The fragmentation is already visible inside the product

Vanta has already tried to answer "show me what I need to do," three separate times. My Work, My Security Tasks, and My Access Requests are three disconnected surfaces. That's the problem in miniature: without a shared primitive, every team that needs a "here's your work" view builds its own.

As Vanta has grown, controls, tests, policies, access reviews, vendor risk, risk registers, people management, questionnaires, each surface developed its own view of the work to be done. They share elements like an owner and a status, but there's no common harness tying them together.

Four problems are well understood, and we're rallying around them. We'd also name a fifth.

Given · 01

Cognitive overload

No single place to see what's due across surfaces.

Given · 02

No prioritization

No guidance on what to tackle first.

Given · 03

Poor delegation

Work gets assigned over Slack, email, and spreadsheets.

Given · 04

No leadership visibility

No view of workload, bottlenecks, or audit-readiness.

Identified · 05

Bespoke workflow drift

Without a central system, Vanta teams will keep building one-off action workflows, compounding the fragmentation with every new surface. This is the platform problem underneath the other four.

Why this matters, and why now

The more a customer leans on Vanta, the harder the work gets to manage.

Picture the person who owns compliance at a 300-person company, mid-audit. Every morning they check Tests, then Access Reviews, then Policies, then Vendors, then Documents, each with its own idea of "what needs attention."

Vanta detects the issues and even has products that inform remediation steps across different paths (CLI, Terraform, and more). But the person accountable for the program has no single place to easily see all the actions that need to happen now, next, and later.

Expanding usage shouldn't make the work harder to manage. Today it does, because staying on top of it requires the customer to be the integration layer.

Why now

Critical mass

The surface count is high enough that fragmentation now compounds with every new feature.

Foundation exists

The Vanta Agent already reasons across surfaces. What's missing is a place to act on it.

Renewal risk

Work piling up is a leading indicator of a program losing momentum, and of churn.

Directional assumptions we'd validate
These are the signals we'd expect to confirm now is the moment, not established fact: that overdue-item frequency is trending up, that the inefficiency is voiced in renewal conversations, and that it threatens the continuous-trust promise. We'd confirm them first (covered in How we'd validate the assumptions).
What success looks like

How we'd expect the world to look if we succeed.

01

Work matches the org

Customers manage work in the ways that fit them. We meet teams where they are, rather than forcing everyone into our flow.

02

Less time managing

Fewer clicks and less time hunting across surfaces, chasing people, and bookkeeping in spreadsheets.

03

No future drift

Reusable primitives mean the next surface plugs into the same foundation instead of inventing its own.

Vision & Strategy

Intent: align on our approach to tackling this, the vision, who we serve, the journey, the bets we'd validate, how we measure, and the product itself.

Vision & mission

Pulse is a command center for compliance work, in service of Vanta's mission.

A single surface where work from across Vanta is consolidated, and where each user gets a contextual view shaped by the kind of work they're responsible for.

Vanta's mission is to help businesses earn and prove trust, and the way Vanta does that is by making compliance continuous. Continuous only works if the work actually gets done. That's the thread Pulse pulls on.

Vanta's mission
Help businesses earn and prove trust.
How
By making compliance continuous, not point-in-time.
Which depends on
The right work getting done, on time, without falling through the cracks.
Pulse's mission (working draft)
Give every team one complete, trustworthy view of their compliance work, so they can stay continuously focused on it instead of chasing it across surfaces.
Who we're building for

We segment by relationship to the work, not by role.

To advocate for the right needs, we used PARK (Perform, Approve, Review, Know) combined with jobs-to-be-done. We don't segment by role because responsibilities don't map neatly to roles: one person can perform some work, approve other work, and just need awareness of the rest.

RelationshipThe job-to-be-done
PerformAs a user performing remediation actions identified by Vanta, I need clarity on what work comes first and what action to take.
ApproveAs a user approving work done by others, I need to confirm the people I assigned work to have actually completed it.
ReviewAs a user reviewing the security posture of my company, I need to give input where it's required without managing every item.
KnowAs a user accountable for the program, I need confidence we're on track without having to manage the work myself.

The same lens, made concrete

John at AcmeApprove + Review

Owns the program. Manages everything inside Vanta but drives execution through Slack and email, including to people who'll never log in. His defining need is the one no one else has: to see what he's doing, what he's delegated out, and whether it's getting done, even when the doing happens somewhere Vanta can't see. He is our primary user.

The engineering team at Nick's KicksPerform

All have Vanta accounts. Mostly performing the work. They want a clean queue of what's theirs and how to resolve it. They are not tracking anyone else's work.

Mike at AttentiveKnow

Works through a task-tracker integration, but today that's scoped narrowly to tests. Evidence the delegation story exists but is incomplete, and a reminder that the performer often lives outside Vanta entirely.

Who we're deliberately not building for

We aren't building for the employees at our customers who engage with Vanta outside of PARK, like the non-technical teams who get an email to accept a policy. They don't need a command center; their flows continue as they are today.

How we'd validate the assumptions

Test the bets that justify the investment.

Before committing, we'd confirm the directional assumptions behind the Why Now.

Instrument the trend

Pull existing data on overdue frequency and time-to-resolution. Is the problem getting worse, and for whom?

Talk to Approve users

Interview accountable owners across SMB and enterprise on how they orchestrate today, and what lives in Slack and spreadsheets.

Mine renewal & CS signal

Is "we can't keep work moving" showing up in renewal conversations and support tickets?

Other inputs we'd seek

Three inputs would most change the plan:

  • The full catalogue of action types across surfaces. It tells us whether the contract's fields are actually sufficient, or whether a class of work won't fit, which would reshape the abstraction before we build on it.
  • Usage data on the three existing "my work" surfaces. Who uses them and where they fall short sets the bar Pulse has to clear, and tells us whether consolidation is a real win or just a reshuffle.
  • The task-tracker integration's current scope and roadmap. External execution is central to the model; if that integration is expanding or constrained, it changes how aggressively we lean on "the performer lives outside Vanta."
How we'll know what good looks like

One goal, one star, guardrails.

Goal

Make sure the compliance work that matters gets done on time, without forcing teams out of the workflows they already use, on a shared foundation that scales as Vanta adds surfaces.

North Star
% of high-priority actions resolved before their due date
An outcome, not activity. Binary at the item level. Counts a resolution no matter where it happened, so it respects "don't force teams into our flow."
Supporting
Reduction in time / clicks managing work
Measures whether the orchestrator's job actually got easier.
Guardrail
Notification volume & mute rate
On-time resolution should rise while notification volume stays flat or falls, not by notifying more aggressively.

As we build, we'll also watch adoption of actions by product area, a build-time check that the foundation is being taken up.

The product

A contextual to-do list, built on a shared action contract.

Pulse is organized around actions: discrete, trackable pieces of work, explicitly not notifications. It shows all of a user's work in one place, and layers systems on top to surface what matters most. It has three parts.

Part 1

Action Interface

The consolidated, prioritized, contextual view of actions. The foundation everything else builds on.

Part 2

AI Context

The Agent's cross-surface intelligence embedded in Pulse: prioritization, the fix attached, assisted resolution.

Part 3

Insights

A view into work completed over time: bottlenecks, workload, audit-readiness. Confidence without managing every item.

A note on why AI comes before Insights: we already have an engine, the Agent, that can reason across surfaces and accelerate this work. The discipline is not to slap it in before we've defined the action contract and the to-do list structure it operates on. AI is most valuable once it has a clean, consistent set of actions to prioritize and resolve against, which is exactly what Part 1 establishes.

How it relates to existing notification systems

A notification service exists today; we introduce an action service. Pulse consolidates actions. The two are deliberately decoupled:

  • An action can trigger a notification, or not. Actions are persistent and live in Pulse; notifications are events that point back to them.
  • A notification doesn't always mean a new action.

That separation is how we balance urgency against fatigue: critical work alerts immediately, the long tail is batched, so low-priority items don't generate the same notification volume.

The mental model: assessing fit

Three form factors are worth weighing for a surface like this. We explored each and assessed its fit for the problem.

ModelAssessment of fit
Email inboxImplies read-and-archive. Compliance items aren't read, they're resolved, and an ignored item is a liability.
FeedOptimizes for awareness, not action. Too passive for work that demands completion.
Task trackerClosest fit, but implies Vanta owns execution and replaces tools teams already use.

Pulse is a contextual to-do list: it shows what needs attention, who's responsible, and how to resolve it, while staying intentionally distinct from a Linear or Jira.

It's adjacent to a task tracker, but it isn't one. We integrate with those tools rather than replace them, customers use Pulse and their tracker together. Pulse is the source of truth for what needs attention; it stays agnostic about where the doing happens. That respects the John-at-Acme reality instead of fighting it.

The platform spine: one shared contract

Pulse isn't a new surface that aggregates objects. It's a shared capability defined by a contract, because every Vanta object already shares a near-common shape: an owner, a status, and an implied action. Any surface that conforms to the contract appears in Pulse with no bespoke work.

One system, contextual views

Underneath, one contract. On top, the view adapts to the user's relationship to the work: John sees what he owns and what he's delegated out; the Nick's Kicks engineers see a clean personal queue. We considered fully role-specific inboxes and chose against them, they'd reintroduce, at the role level, the fragmentation the contract exists to prevent.

Scaling down-market and up-market

Down-market, one person could hold every PARK relationship at once: the job is "tell me what to do next," and the win is a single opinionated queue that removes decisions. Up-market, the relationships split across people and teams: the job shifts from doing the work to orchestrating and proving it, and the win is delegation, tracking, and a defensible view for an auditor. Same contract, different center of gravity.

One concrete implication: the North Star reads differently by segment. Down-market, "high-priority actions resolved on time" may not suffer from the cordination costs we see upmarket. The same metric can apply, but up-market is more of a measure of process health, which we should keep in mind

Part III

How we get there

Intent: align on now / next / later priorities. We'll re-evaluate continuously, but lay out the broad sequencing based on what we know today.

The roadmap

Three pillars, sequenced into Now, Next, and Later.

Pulse is built from three pillars. They aren't strictly phased, we'd pull the highest-value slice of each into every stage rather than finishing one before starting the next.

Pillar 1

Action Interface

The contract, the consolidated contextual view, and the resolution model. The foundation.

Pillar 2

AI Context

The Agent's cross-surface intelligence: prioritization, the fix attached, assisted resolution.

Pillar 3

Insights

Completion over time, workload, bottlenecks, audit-readiness.

NowProve the foundation
  • Core Pillar 1: define and ship the action contract and action service; the contextual view that adapts by relationship; the resolution model; decouple from the notification service
  • MVP slice: consolidate My Work / My Security Tasks / My Access Requests onto the contract before extending to new object types
  • A thin slice of Pillar 2: experiment with AI as part of the interface (e.g. computed prioritization), enough to make the queue useful, not the full engine

The smallest slice that proves the bet. Modeling the three existing "my work" surfaces as actions behind one contract tests the abstraction itself, not new surfaces. If one contract represents all three without per-surface special-casing, the shared-primitive bet holds. If it can't, we've learned that for the cost of a consolidation, not a platform.

The risk if we sequence wrong. Extending to new object types before the contract is proven means discovering each needs bespoke fields, the exact fragmentation we set out to eliminate, now baked into the platform. Proving the contract on what exists first is the guardrail.

NextDeepen and extend
  • Deeper Pillar 1 (the non-MVP work): extend the contract to new object types (tests and documents first, then issues, access reviews, policies); user customization and control over the view; team and delegated-ownership handling; configurable rules for up-market
  • Pillar 2 iteration: move AI from prioritization into assisted resolution and the fix-attached experience
  • Basic Pillar 3: first completion-tracking and program-status views, once enough surfaces emit clean actions to make a rollup trustworthy

What Next depends on Now delivering. Insights is only trustworthy if the action data underneath is complete and consistently shaped. Next can't start until Now has enough surfaces emitting clean actions that a program-level rollup reflects reality.

LaterMature the surface
  • Deep Pillar 3: rich in-product insights, bottleneck analysis, audit-readiness forecasting
  • Mature Pillar 2: proactive, agent-driven resolution across surfaces
  • Full enterprise orchestration surface area on top of the same contract
Enabling Vanta Features to Plug In

The hard part is adoption, so we make conforming to the contract nearly free.

A shared primitive only works if teams actually adopt it, so the architecture and the org approach are both designed to lower that cost.

Adoption & architecture

  • Low cost to adopt. For a team like Tests or Policies, conforming is a thin adapter mapping fields they already have (owner, status, due date) onto the contract, plus emitting actions as state changes. Not a schema migration or a rewrite. If it's expensive, teams won't, and the platform fails quietly.
  • Built on what exists. An action service sits alongside the notification service as the system of record. It reads the existing data model (the Agent proves cross-surface reasoning works), and reuses the regionalized delivery fan-out (US / EU / AUS, to SES, Slack, Teams) rather than rebuilding it.
  • Priority is scored centrally. Surfaces supply the raw inputs (due date, audit-blocking status, blast radius); the action service applies one scoring model across all of them. If each team scored its own, "high priority" would fragment, the exact thing the contract prevents.
  • Resolution is observed or attested. Auto-resolve where there's a signal (a tracker status, a test re-run); otherwise the accountable user attests, attestation, not proof.

The trade-off worth naming

Pulse asks surface teams to do adapter work that ships no feature on their own roadmap. The pitch we need to make internally is that by leveraging this primitive they can also accomplish their goals and improve the user experience of their product areas.

Partnership & rollout

We build on the Collaboration team's adjacent primitives rather than over them + we ship in beta: pilot the consolidation of the three existing surfaces with a small cohort, confirm time and clicks drop and on-time resolution holds, then extend to new object types.

Wrap Up

Build Pulse on a shared action contract, and prove it on what already exists.

Our recommendation is deliberate: a contextual to-do list, organized around a reusable action primitive, that consolidates the surfaces Vanta has already built before extending to new ones. It's the approach that solves the four problems we set out against and the fifth we identified, respects how customers actually work, and gives every future Vanta surface a place to land for free.

The reason to build this isn't a better inbox. It's that the cost of not building it is paid again with every new surface.
