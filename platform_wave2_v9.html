Building Stronger Signal
What we mean by signal

Signal means we have confidence that we have the inputs needed to make a well-informed prioritization decision. It doesn't mean a focus area is definitively the right bet — it means we have enough context from data, customer conversations, and cross-functional perspective to make a call we can stand behind. Right now, three of the six areas are at strong signal. The remaining three are in progress. We need the full picture across all six before we can recommend a prioritized order.

Where we are

We're still building cross-functional alignment across the BU lead roles. That's harder right now because each of us is coming in with a different depth of background in these areas. The product lead has been immersed in these problems for a while — but that context hasn't fully transferred yet, which is part of what this process is for. The goal is a shared foundation before we commit to a prioritization order.

Focus Area 01

First 90 Days

CS ScaleSKU Scale
↑ All areas
Hypothesis

A structured, contextual intake before and after kickoff will reduce downstream CS tickets — and, by reducing the prep burden per call, allow CS to run more kickoff calls effectively without burning out.

Additional context

CS is already proving this manually — today they send a Google Form link plus an email with 7+ helpsite links before every kickoff call. Customers who go through that flow arrive better prepared and generate fewer follow-up tickets. A product-native version replaces that manual work and scales it. Current signal on this area is mixed: there's limited evidence it moves the sales funnel directly, but the POC angle is underexplored — structured onboarding is operationally expensive for CS and a high-leverage part of the funnel where better tooling could meaningfully improve close rates. The key question is whether the data confirms this is primarily a CS efficiency story, a conversion story, or both.

What it is

Making the first 90 days with Adaptive more proactive, personalized, and operationally lighter for CS — without eliminating the kickoff call. The kickoff is valuable. The goal is to arrive prepared and reduce post-call support volume.

Discovery · What we need to learn

  • CS interviews on current manual workarounds and prep burden per kickoff call
  • Analyze early-tenure support tickets to understand where customers actually get stuck — this is the most important input for moving signal from "building" to "strong"
  • Research how frequently admins are added outside of the kickoff window (30+ days post-kickoff) to validate the impact of individual-level onboarding guidance at scale
  • Assess POC operational cost and close rate data — the hypothesis that structured onboarding improves POC conversion is the most compelling business case and needs validation
  • Key questions: Is friction a product or education problem? Does the data show this is CS efficiency, POC conversion, or both?

How things change if we're successful

  • Tickets per customer go down — CS scales more proportionally to the account base without needing to grow headcount at the same rate
  • Admins added post-kickoff don't generate their own wave of "how do I…" tickets — individual-level onboarding becomes a product motion, not a CS motion
  • POC conversion improves — we expect to see materially higher conversion rates on POCs that go through structured onboarding flows vs. those that don't, which becomes a repeatable lever

Customers who've gone through the manual version arrive in materially better shape. This automates that entirely.

Customers add new admins throughout their lifecycle — those admins never had a kickoff call and often generate their own wave of CS questions. A product-native first-login flow guides them through the product areas most relevant to their role without CS involvement.

Focus Area 02

Attributes & Identity

ParityCS ScaleSKU Scale
↑ All areas
Hypothesis

CS is spending a disproportionate amount of time managing attribute friction because our attribute layer doesn't meet customer expectations — and the gap will get more acute as attributes become foundational to autofish, new SKUs, and the Email Security integration.

Additional context

We've expanded custom attribute support across some integrations and via CSV — which introduced the concept — but coverage is incomplete across our integration surface and we don't support derived fields, inline editing, or remapping without eng intervention. The user profile page has accumulated these capabilities as bandaids rather than a cohesive design, and it lacks the value customers expect to get from it. Current read is that this is a significant point of friction rather than a hard blocker — customers work around it, which makes the ROI harder to quantify than a deal-stopper. The Intercom ticket data is the key input: if attribute-related tickets represent a meaningful share of CS load, the efficiency case becomes clear. The new SKU argument (Email Security, Risk) is a second vector that may prove stronger than the direct customer-facing one.

What it is

We've expanded attribute capabilities but largely put bandaids on the user profile page — it lacks the coherence and depth customers want from it. This is about closing the coverage gap across integrations, adding the primitives we're missing (derived fields, inline editing, remapping), and rebuilding the profile into something customers actually find valuable. Every downstream investment — automation, personalization, risk scoring, new SKU infrastructure — runs on this data layer.

Discovery · What we need to learn

  • Intercom ticket taxonomy: how much CS load is actually attributable to attribute friction? This is the primary input for determining ROI — vibes suggest it's significant, but the data needs to confirm it
  • Customer problem hunting: what use cases can't we support because we lack non-CSV attribute handling? E.g. zero custom attributes from Microsoft — what deals or expansions has that gap cost us?
  • Review Republic, Trintech CSV upload discovery for existing signal on what customers tried to do that the product couldn't support
  • Early conversation with Email Security team — the new SKU argument (a solid attribute foundation is required for Risk and Email) may prove a stronger case than the direct CS friction angle
  • Key questions: Is this primarily a CS efficiency story, a SKU enablement story, or do both hold up?

How things change if we're successful

  • Attribute friction disappears from CS ticket volume — admins self-serve corrections that today require CSV uploads or eng tickets
  • Downstream investments (automation, personalization, risk scoring) become viable because the data layer is reliable and extensible
  • Email Security and future SKUs bring their own attributes into a mature model instead of working around it
  • The profile drawer becomes the operational surface for managing an employee's full security posture

Focus Area 03

ARV Availability, Reliability & Visibility

ParityCS ScaleSKU Scale
↑ All areas
Hypothesis

Without ClickHouse as a shared, reliable data layer, we can't guarantee consistent group builder behavior across SKUs. Without defined data freshness SLAs, automation rules that depend on data timing aren't trustworthy. And without visibility into notification delivery, CS escalates to eng for answers they should be able to get themselves.

Additional context

Three connected problems: (1) Availability — ClickHouse is the only place we can guarantee all SKU data will be, but the group builder doesn't run against it yet, making cross-SKU segmentation unreliable. (2) Reliability — the platform promises "immediate" data but delivers variable refresh cycles (4–5 min, trending upward) with no defined SLA and no measurement. (3) Visibility — when platform notifications (SendGrid) fail, CS has no self-serve visibility and must escalate to eng. The new SKU group condition problem is a when, not an if — reporting performance is already degrading as we grow and the trajectory is clear. Phishing notifications (Mailgun, SAT BU) are out of scope here.

What it is

Establishing ClickHouse as the authoritative shared data layer for the platform, replacing implicit variable behavior with explicit, measurable commitments — and giving CS visibility into notification delivery so they can self-serve answers without engineering tickets.

Discovery · What we need to learn

  • Email Security team conversation on ClickHouse data requirements and what needs to land there before their launch
  • Engineering assessment of CDC variability trajectory and cost of decoupling
  • CS survey on notification-related tickets — which visibility gaps drive the most escalations?
  • Key questions: What SLA commitment is right — not real-time, but what exactly? Which CS tools would eliminate the most notification tickets?

How things change if we're successful

  • CS can self-serve "did this notification send?" without an engineering ticket — a meaningful slice of escalation volume disappears
  • The platform can make and keep data freshness commitments — automation rules that depend on data timing become trustworthy
  • New SKUs build on ClickHouse as a shared primitive instead of standing up their own data layer
  • Internal account access becomes time-bounded and scoped, reducing the blast radius of a compromised account and strengthening our security posture story
Illustrative Projects · ✦ includes mockup

As more SKUs are added, ClickHouse is the only place we can guarantee all data will be consistently available. Moving the group builder to query ClickHouse is a prerequisite for reliable segmentation at scale and a shared primitive that multi-tenancy and automation logic can depend on. Most foundational ARV project — unlocks multiple downstream investments.

The platform promises "immediate" data availability but has no defined SLA and no measurement of whether we're meeting it. This project defines the actual freshness commitment, instruments measurement, and creates visibility for engineering and CS. A prerequisite for making credible data promises to customers and building automation rules that depend on data freshness.

Current data freshness is coupled to CDC variability, which is getting worse as customer and data volume grows. An append-only event architecture decouples freshness from CDC, making the data layer more predictable and scalable. Foundation for the data SLA work and for reliable ClickHouse replication as new SKU data is added.

When training notifications or system emails fail, CS currently has limited visibility — generating escalations that require engineering investigation. This project creates CS-accessible tooling that surfaces SendGrid delivery status, bounce reasons, and notification history without an eng ticket.

Focus Area 04

Multi-tenancy

ParitySKU Scale
↑ All areas
Hypothesis

Named enterprise deals are being blocked or held together with engineering workarounds because we lack a scalable parent/child account model — and roll-up reporting is likely the minimum viable unlock without requiring a full architecture build upfront.

Additional context

This is one of the clearest cases in the six areas — both existing and prospective customers need this, not just want it, and they tend to be the largest accounts. The absence is a blocker, not friction: deals are being lost or stitched together with engineering workarounds. Estimated pipeline impact is ~$600K/year in incoming opportunities. MSP is a related but substantially harder problem (~8–10 inbound requests/week) and should be scoped as a separate bet. The full multi-tenancy architecture needs to be sequenced carefully relative to the Email Security roadmap — enterprise roll-up reporting may be the right interim step to unblock near-term pipeline without betting on the full architecture prematurely.

What it is

Enabling the platform to support customers with complex org structures — large global enterprises with parent/child relationships, and eventually MSPs/MSSPs managing multiple end customers (separate bet).

Discovery · What we need to learn

  • Sales review of named enterprise accounts — what exactly are the blockers and which deals are held together with eng workarounds?
  • Engineering assessment of roll-up reporting scope vs. full parent/child architecture
  • Email Security team conversation on sequencing dependencies
  • Key questions: What's the minimum viable version to unblock near-term pipeline? Does the full architecture need to be solved before Email Security?

How things change if we're successful

  • Named enterprise accounts in pipeline unblock — no more eng workarounds per deal
  • Parent org admins see aggregate program health across subsidiaries without managing separate logins
  • Adaptive can credibly pursue global enterprise deals that today we have to decline or stitch together manually
  • MSP is treated as the separate bet it is — with a clear on-ramp once enterprise is working

Focus Area 05

AI Agent

CS ScaleSKU Scale
↑ All areas
Hypothesis

The existing AI assistant is underdeveloped because the product surface it would act on — reliable attributes, defined automations, a consistent data layer — doesn't yet exist. Building those primitives first will make the agent meaningfully more powerful when we invest in it.

Additional context

The AI agent is seen as the clearest "platformy" approach to the automation problem — a shared primitive that benefits every BU rather than a point solution. There are three directions we could take: (1) build a framework that helps other BUs automate (a genuinely good assistant), (2) focus on Platform product areas like reporting and risk score, or (3) a cross-cutting initiative that automates key parts of the platform directly. In the automation survey, customers ranked the AI assistant last (14%) — consistent with it being a workaround today rather than a first choice. The right sequencing is primitives first, agent as the interface second. Naming it now ensures those investments are sequenced with the agent vision in mind.

What it is

Developing the existing AI admin assistant into a genuine platform primitive — grounded in product logic, customer data, and help content, capable of taking meaningful actions on an admin's behalf.

Discovery · What we need to learn

  • Audit current assistant usage — what are people actually asking it and where does it consistently fail?
  • Could a grounded agent cover the support gap created by not enabling Fin (Intercom's AI agent)? Understand what Fin would have handled and whether a product-native agent can do it with better context
  • Map which agentic actions become most useful once automation primitives exist
  • Engineering assessment of grounding infrastructure requirements
  • Key questions: What does the agent do in its first genuinely useful version? How dependent is its value on attribute and automation surface maturity?

How things change if we're successful

  • The long tail of tasks too varied for point-and-click automation gets handled — the agent covers what rules can't
  • Adaptive Cowork becomes a product surface other SKUs can route to — not a standalone feature
  • When a customer adds a new SKU, they get agent knowledge for that surface essentially for free
  • Admins get grounded answers to config-specific questions ("why didn't this send?") without escalating to CS — and the agent sounds like a human, not a bot
Illustrative Projects · ✦ includes mockup

The current assistant isn't grounded in product logic or customer-specific configuration. A grounded support assistant is trained on product docs, help content, and the customer's actual config state — so it can answer "why didn't my campaign send?" with a real answer, not a generic response. The most self-contained version of this investment and the least dependent on automation primitives being mature.

Focus Area 06

Automation

ParityCS Scale
↑ All areas
Hypothesis

Admins are spending 2–5+ hours per month on predictable, signal-driven tasks the platform could handle — and the large majority want the platform to recommend and prepare actions, with a human approving before anything runs.

Additional context

Automation as a theme has strong, consistent signal — the question for Platform is which direction to take it. Three options: (1) build a framework that other BUs automate on top of, (2) focus on Platform-specific product areas like reporting and risk score, or (3) cross-cutting automations for key platform workflows like campaigns and remediation. Feb 2026 survey (n=69): importance averaged 8.8/10, 90% rated 8+. The preferred mode was "recommend and run with approvals" (58%) — only a minority want fully automated execution. Remediation program setup after a phishing fail has the highest comfort with more automation (59%), making it the right starting point. This is a retention and CS load story: 45% wouldn't pay more for it, so it's not a pricing play.
